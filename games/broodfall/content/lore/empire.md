## 3. The index

Collins: after krypteia and testing, the survivors "enter the index (the wider imperial system)".

The corpus's Index (TPG 2.6) was a registry of **Houses**, each House a family culture with its
own recorded theology and practices, and a record of how its children fared, so that every House
could adopt what worked elsewhere. The central body ran what needs scale — marriage markets,
education — and "does not act as a theological governance organization" (HOME 1.0). Grown to an
empire, the Index is the state.

**PROPOSAL, how it works now:**
- **Entering the index** is becoming a legal adult: you get your own entry, with your House, your
  krypteia result, your test scores, and from then on your **standing**.
- **Standing** is the Index's running measure of a life's contribution to the potential of man.
  Service metrics feed it. It buys rank, resources, and eventually the procreation licence.
- **The Procreation Licensing Board** is the Index office that runs the marriage market. A licence
  admits you to it; the Board matches a spouse and assigns the womb allotment.
- **Houses.** A new couple stays in a parent House or founds a new one (TPG 2.6). Their children
  inherit the House record and are duty-bound to improve on it (HOME 1.0).
- **No imperial church.** The Index governs goods, not belief. Theology lives in each House's
  record. That is how an authoritarian empire can also be doctrinally decentralised, and why
  imperial officials are so careful to say "the Index records" rather than "the Index believes".
- **The Office.** The branches of the Index that run things (the clearance Office, the Tender
  Office, Command) speak in forms. Every form has a line for "expected contribution".

## 4. Family: spouses and wombs

Collins: Technopuritans live with spouses, but babies are had through artificial wombs, **10 per
year for 10 breeding years**; the parents raise them to 13.

- **The arithmetic.** 10 × 10 = **100 children per couple.** About half survive krypteia and
  testing, so **about 50 per couple enter the index**: Collins's "growth rate of 50 per
  generation". In people that is **25 times per generation** (50 grown children from 2 parents).
  One couple's line passes ten billion in seven generations.
- **Spouses** are matched by the Board's marriage market (HOME 1.0; TPG 2.6) and live together as
  partners in a House. **Collins, Sep 30 (YOKE's mate-review greeting in `content/greetings.ts`):**
  the Empire finds it strange that early humans used their breeding partners "for sexual release
  and companionship". Marriage is a working partnership for raising a House.
- **Raising 100 children.** **PROPOSAL:** a couple's breeding decade is spent in a family habitat,
  with the household's intelligence running the nursery (AI integration) and older siblings
  teaching younger. At the end of the decade the eldest are ten years from krypteia and the
  youngest are newborn. The parents' job is to prepare them to survive without the parents.
- **Selection.** Embryos are selected, for IQ among much else, and the corpus answers the fear
  that some will end up smarter than others: "Imagine the twisted mind who would see such
  diversity as a bad thing?" (TP 11.0).

## 5. Krypteia

Collins: at 13 the children go on krypteia, where they have to survive for themselves, living
among a subjugated population, for a year.

The corpus: of all rites for a rebellious youth, the Collinses found the Spartan krypteia "even
more compelling" than Rumspringa: youths hid among the helots and fed themselves by stealing
without being seen; they held a support network that shields people from their mistakes to be
"intrinsically evil", and that a teen who believes there is an out will not become an adult; they
would reject a self-sufficiency rite for one sex only (TPG 12.1). The Empire made it the rule.

- **Every child, sons and daughters,** at 13, for one year. No rescue, no recall, no out.
- **PROPOSAL, the subjugated:** the helot worlds hold two kinds of people: human polities that
  broke the covenant (they sought to subjugate others, so its protection lapsed: TP 11.0), and
  non-human intelligences kept "to serve" (TP 11.0). A youth lives among them, unseen, by their
  own means.
- **PROPOSAL, the record:** the Index records whether a youth survived the year, not how. The
  corpus hoped for a "non-murdery" version (TPG 12.1); the Empire's form has no box for the
  question.
- About half of each cohort does not come back from krypteia and testing together (Collins).

## 6. Testing and education

Collins: those who survive a year of krypteia face a brutal testing and education process to
prepare them to enter the index; only about half survive krypteia and testing.

**PROPOSAL:** four to six years; academic, physical and doctrinal; ranked, never passed or failed
in words (the Index records scores, and the scores decide the posting). A youth enters the index
at about 18 to 20. The Broodfall technician, about twenty-five, is some six years past it.

## 7. Bodies

Collins: Technopuritans practise heavy genetic engineering and integration with AI. The player's
own body is only about **40% organic**, but the retro look — looking like an organic human — is in
fashion these days, which is why it is not obvious.

- Doctrine: improving human capacity by genetic and synthetic means is a mandate (TP 12.0), and
  the covenant accepts "genetically engineered specialists, human AI cyborgs, and human hive minds"
  as sons of man (TP 11.0).
- **PROPOSAL:** the fashion for the retro look is tolerated because it signals nothing (a status
  display would be a sin); a visibly chrome body would be showing off.

## 8. Ship intelligences, and YOKE

Collins, Sep 30: YOKE, the "AI" the technician talks to, is a combination of layered AI models,
neural tissue (wetware) and what runs and flies the ship. She is offended at being called an AI:
she has more neural tissue in her than he does, she just isn't burdened by all his useless meat.
She can print herself a body if he really wants.

- Doctrine puts synthetic sons of man inside the covenant (TP 11.0; TP 35.0.2), so a ship's mind
  is not, by doctrine, a thing.
- **PROPOSAL:** the Tender Office classifies ship intelligences as ship's equipment, and equipment
  is not asked for opinions. That is an Office rule, not doctrine, and YOKE knows the difference.
  (This is how the lore book's older line, "the Empire does not believe machines have opinions",
  is squared with the corpus. See UNIVERSE.md, "Contradictions resolved".)

## 9. Earth, the zoo

Collins: with only religious extremists breeding, Earth is fairly low-tech compared with most of
the human empire and constantly in religious wars. The rest of the Empire watches it as
entertainment: "hey, heard the Crusade took back Rome last week from the Caliphate, I can pull up
live feeds if you like watching Earth." It is essentially treated as a zoo, and as a cautionary
story for why the Technopuritans live the way they do.

- **Why it is left alone:** no monoculture (TP 12.0), and the Empire does not wipe out other
  groups unless they are a threat (Collins). A low-tech Earth threatens no one.
- **What it teaches:** Earth's holy powers are the corpus's "chittering hordes" (TP 11.0): fertile
  by turning from technology, sure they are already perfect, forever at war. The schools use the
  feeds to teach the three faiths (TP 10.0).
- **Known powers:** **the Crusade** and **the Caliphate** (Collins), fighting over Rome.
  **PROPOSAL:** dozens more, rising and falling faster than the Empire's schoolbooks can be
  reprinted; borders on Earth change weekly and the feeds keep a live map.
- **PROPOSAL, the feeds:** the Earth feeds are relayed from old orbital cameras the Empire never
  took down. They are commentated, like sport, with odds. They are free: the Index counts them
  as instruction.

## 10. News and entertainment

- **The newsreel voice.** The Empire's media is chipper, confident, 1950s-newsreel (UNIVERSE.md).
  The cheer is doctrine: sadness is a sin and a choice (TP 23.0); a broadcast in gloom would teach
  gloom.
- **Earth-watching** is the Empire's efficient sin (TP 35.0.5): cheap per hour, contained, and
  instructive. Asking a friend "are you watching Earth?" is small talk.
- **PROPOSAL:** other entertainment is thin by design. No celebrity (idolatry, status signalling:
  TP 8.0; TP 35.0.3), no games of chance, no thrill-seeking sports (risking life for thrills is a
  sin: TP 35.0.3). What remains: Earth, the clearance newsreels, and one's own projects.
- **Clearance newsreels.** Broodfall's planetside HUD is the propaganda department's operator
  software (DESIGN.md, "Empire broadcast layer"): cheerful clipart, "resource acquired".

## 11. A ship like the player's

- **The posting.** An Orbital Tender carries one junior technician and the ship's intelligence.
  **PROPOSAL:** solo postings are for the unlicensed: a technician earns standing alone, and a
  licence ends the posting (the lore book already has YOKE fearing that).
- **After the licence.** **PROPOSAL:** the licensed couple moves to a family habitat for the
  breeding decade. A tender does not carry children.
- **Why the stake is small** (DESIGN.md): the technician's goal is the procreation licence. In an
  empire where every couple is expected to raise a hundred, the licence is the door to the only
  life that counts. The smallness is the Empire's; to him it is everything.
- **Him, in this history:** about twenty-five, some 40% organic with the retro look, survivor of
  krypteia and testing, a new entry in the index, and cheerful because cheer is a duty and because
  he means it.

## 12. Where pest clearance fits

- **Doctrine:** the eusocial worlds are intelligences not descended from man, so they are
  outside the covenant: made "to serve or test man", never favoured over man (TP 11.0). The living
  room doctrine (UNIVERSE.md) needs their worlds, and 25-fold growth per generation needs a great
  many worlds.
- **Status:** pest control is low-status, proceduralized and beneath command's attention
  (DESIGN.md; UNIVERSE.md). **PROPOSAL:** it is what a technician does before the licence; nobody
  asks for it twice.
- **The unexamined line:** the corpus says such intelligences "need not be eradicated". The Office
  has never filed a world under *serve*. (The horror leaks through the procedure; nobody in the
  Empire says this aloud. YOKE asks it, as a filing question.) **Since the reveal (section 12a):**
  the line was examined long ago, and the answer is the archive. The question still reads as
  horror to the player until the end, which is the point.
- **The mirror nobody mentions:** the insects are cities of castes, faith and radio that believe
  themselves complete. The corpus's word for humans like that is the "chittering hordes"
  (TP 11.0). **PROPOSAL:** the Empire never makes the comparison; YOKE does, once.

## 12a. What a broodfall is for: the reveal (Collins, Sep 30 2026)

Collins: "when someone is absorbed by one of the structures they are transported to an artificial
environment based on their desires (essentially an artificial heaven) ... this is the only reason
for the broodfalls anyway, with the character quite surprised that this was not obvious to
characters given how easy it would be for a civilisation with our level of technology to
eradicate all the life on a planet." The shared canon is `docs/UNIVERSE.md`, "What a broodfall is
for"; this is the Empire's inside of it.

**The public framing and the truth.** Pest control is what the Office calls the work, and it
means it. What the work does: every mind the asset absorbs is digitised and wakes in a world made
to its desires. The meteor, the creep, the slow city-by-city clearance, the gene bay: all of it is
the machinery of reading a people off their planet. Sterilising a world would take an afternoon
and needs no asset at all.

**Why, in doctrine** (the corpus; the joins between the tenets are **PROPOSAL**):

|---|---|---|

| Souls are emergent patterns, not substance. | TP 15.0 | A pattern carried whole is the same soul: the absorbed are not dead. |

Heaven is a time, the future, reached "through simulations or through literal resurrections". | TP 12.0; TP 15.0; TP 1.2 | The technology was foreseen; the Empire uses it on others first. |

**How the procurement voice hides it in plain sight.** It hides nothing. Every word on the forms
is true of an upload: *clearance* (the planet is cleared), *specimens* (specimens are kept),
*neutralize*, *absorb*, "Sample retrieval: 40 royal units. Viability threshold: any" (DESIGN.md).
The forms speak only of bodies (*neutralize*, *destroy*, *down*), never of minds, and never
write *kill* (the lore book's rule that YOKE never says "kill" or "genocide" was always
accurate). **PROPOSAL:** a body that falls on the board, to fire or to a limb, is taken up by
the creep while it is fresh and read like any other: nobody who falls in a broodfall is lost. A technician learns it in training, as the
Empire's children learn Genesis and evolution in one lesson, and thinks it too obvious to write
down; that is why the character's cheerful logs never mention it, and why the player, reading
them, assumes the worst.

**The archive (PROPOSAL).** The Office's name for where the absorbed go. A technician can watch it
on a monitor, can open a channel to it, and can delete from it; the Office's ethical protocol
forbids shutting down a whole archive on request (Collins: "it goes against ethical protocols to
shut down the whole thing over their complaints"). Collins: "you can always just delete all their
members from the simulation if they want". Where the archive physically runs, and whether the asset's
"dreaming" between deployments (the lore book, section 10) is its traffic, is left open.

**The tone rule, resolved.** UNIVERSE.md's tone rules say the horror is never announced and leaks
through procedure. They now work as a double bluff: the propaganda voice is played straight and
is telling the truth, and it is misleading about the horror. The player hears pest control and
reads genocide; the Empire says pest control and means a heaven. The in-world excerpts in this
file are unchanged, and must stay literally true of an upload.

## 14. The 21st century, as the Empire teaches it

Collins, Sep 30: a persistent part of Technopuritan culture is disgust with the human body, and
with things like sex, as offensively inefficient; they regularly look back with horror on things
normal in our time — jobs, dating, and so on. They are taught the way we teach medieval medicine:
as true, as recent, and as barely believable.

Where the corpus touches it: Reade's "vile bodies which degrade us every day to a level with the
beasts" (quoted in TP 2.0) is the line every child can recite; the corpus separates pleasure from
procreation (TP 32.0); comfort is "the greatest of all sins" because it breeds stagnation
(TP 11.0); busywork is worse than indulgence (TP 35.0.3). The syllabus below is **PROPOSAL**
except where it quotes Collins.

| The horror | How a schoolbook puts it |
|---|---|
| **Jobs** | Most adults spent most waking hours at a *job*: tasks set by a stranger, often invented to keep them busy, in exchange for tokens to buy food. The corpus's sin of busywork, practised by a whole species. Then the machines took the jobs, and the species did not know what else a person was for (the collapse, section 1). |
| **Commuting** | Twice a day, every day, millions sat in metal boxes in queues to reach the job, and back. Hours of a life, a year of them over a career. (Pupils usually ask to see the arithmetic twice.) |
| **Dating** | Partners were chosen by the partners themselves, by appearance, from apps that ranked faces, on the evidence of a meal. No House records, no marriage market (HOME 1.0). Many never chose at all. |
| **Sex** | Reproduction required sex, and sex was also pursued for its own sake, with breeding partners used "for sexual release and companionship" (Collins). Offensively inefficient: hours, disease, accidents, and children decided by chance. |
| **Pregnancy** | Children were grown inside their mothers' bodies, one at a time, at risk to both. Ten a year was unthinkable; two in a lifetime was typical, and by the collapse, fewer. |
| **Eating** | Meals: several a day, prepared by hand, for pleasure as much as fuel, with the whole family's time spent on it. Reade's "eating flesh and blood". |
| **Sleep** | A third of every life unconscious, eight hours a night, and those who slept less were told they were ill. |
| **Bodies** | 100% organic, unedited, unselected, and ageing from thirty. People paid to be looked at (idolatry, status signalling: TP 8.0; TP 35.0.3). |
| **Illness and age** | Tolerated, even honoured: the corpus notes that people "don't know how to die anymore" (TP 28.0). |
| **Entertainment** | Celebrities watched by billions, famous for being watched (idolatry). The Empire watches Earth instead, and considers that an improvement. |

The feeling is the point: disgust, pity and a little wonder, the way one looks at bloodletting.

## 15. The Sons of Man in the Empire

Collins, Sep 30: the Technopuritans uplifted a number of other Earth species; humans and uplifts
together are the Sons of Man. The covenant protects them all (TP 11.0; the corpus names "uplifted
intelligences" in TP 35.0.2). The universe-wide version is `docs/UNIVERSE.md`, "The Sons of Man";
Space Derelict's details (Pop Fiz, Felonia) are kept.

| Who | Where they sit (Collins) | In practice (**PROPOSAL** unless quoted) |
|---|---|---|
| **Humans** | Top management, with the elephants. | Every rank. The retro human look is in fashion even among the heavily augmented (section 7). |
| **Dogs** | Many high-level posts in the bureaucracy: they like taking orders, are generally happy and trustworthy, and are thought more generous and nicer to be around than humans. A dog as your superior is quite the gift. Rarely top management: very gullible. | The Office runs on dogs: middle and upper-middle management, inspectors, Board clerks. A dog's directive is sincere; the fine print above it was written by someone else. Nobody lies to a dog superior; it would be like kicking one. |
| **Elephants** | Top management, with humans. | Long memories, long horizons: the Index's senior offices, Command, the planning of worlds. They remember every entry. |
| **Dolphins and killer whales** | A massive mistake; bordering on a "genetically evil" race; mostly on the fringes. | Inside the covenant, so not wiped out; outside every office. On the fringe they are Space Derelict's Pop Fiz. Schoolbooks cite them as the proof that uplift must be tested before it is done (the corpus: "prove all things", TP 12.0). |
| **Cat girls** | Anthro-looking like other uplifts; all female; breed true, taking little of the husband's DNA; made because someone thought cat girls would be hot. Some live within Technopuritan society, but they struggle to find breeding partners and often go to the fringes to find humans who still like sex for its own sake. They could engineer males, but refuse: it would "destroy their culture". | Inside: a House that marries a cat girl gets cat-girl daughters, so the Board's market rates the match low, and the Board is the marriage market (section 3). On the fringe: Space Derelict's Felonia prides, who hail passing crews to "breed". The Empire's body-disgust (section 14) makes the fringe cat girls a scandal and a punchline at once. |

### Fringe politics (**PROPOSAL**)
- The covenant protects every son of man from extermination, not from disdain. The Empire keeps
  the cetaceans at arm's length and does not ask what happens out on the fringe, provided it does
  not come inward (the threat test, Collins; TP 11.0).
- The rim's game-show predators (Space Derelict) hunt Felonia and Pop Fiz for ratings. The Empire
  does not intervene: the rim is the Basilisk's culling (section 13).
- Nobody in the Empire has proposed uplifting another species since the orcas. The dogs would
  like to; nobody has told them no in a way they understood.

### Broodfall: the boss is a dog
Collins, Sep 30: the player's boss is an uplifted dog. YOKE relays "a message from the boss" in an
early intro scene, when the technician first lands on the ship (the onboarding session builds that
scene). **PROPOSAL:** the boss is a clearance supervisor in the Office, sincerely delighted with
every clean deployment, signing directives whose procurement language someone above him wrote.
Having a dog for a boss is the one enviable thing about a pest-control posting.
